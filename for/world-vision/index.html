  1. Red flag 01Control shown

    A small-business consultant in an enterprise role

    The concern

    “His clients are small businesses. This is an enterprise transformation role.”

    My answer

    That’s my honest gap: I haven’t shaped AI strategy for an organisation your size. So I’d work through the people who already lead each area: Technology, Data, Risk, Privacy, Legal, Finance, and People & Culture. I’d build the strategy with them, and with the people doing the work.

    The control

    Every initiative gets a business owner and measures agreed up front, and goes through your enterprise governance before it’s funded.

  2. Red flag 02Control shown

    AI pushed into everything

    The concern

    “An AI lead will want to put AI into everything.”

    My answer

    Before any agent, I ask the four questions below. A clear no on any of the first three and I don’t build it. At Mattick Air I sorted each backlog item into build, buy or wait. A logistics client got a pipeline with no AI at run time.

    The four questions

    • Is the input structured?Does it start from something consistent, not a vague one-liner?
    • Are the steps definable?Can the steps be written down, the same way each time?
    • Is the output checkable?Can a person tell quickly whether it’s right?
    • What does a wrong answer cost?Low and reversible: it acts. High or hard to undo: it drafts, a person decides.
    The control

    Work that doesn’t justify central transformation capacity is deferred or stopped. Lower-risk ideas go to business teams to run themselves.

  3. Red flag 03Control shown

    Children’s data and safeguarding

    The concern

    “Our work involves children. The bar for their information is higher than anything his clients hold.”

    My answer

    When the people at the end of the work are children, the bar should be higher again, and agreed before anything is built. Where AI earns a place, it drafts and a person sends, and private information stays private. Safeguarding is your specialists’ field, and I’d build to their bar.

    The control

    Controls in proportion to risk, set with your safeguarding, Privacy and Legal people, and a person who can review, override or switch it off.

  4. Red flag 04Control shown

    A builder who duplicates Technology and Data

    The concern

    “He’s a builder. He’ll end up duplicating our Technology and Data teams.”

    My answer

    My clients are too small to have those teams, so I’d work alongside yours. Every piece of work has a business owner. I compare options before building and write the briefs engineers build from. Where a plainer option is more reliable, I’d choose it.

    The control

    Technology and Data keep their accountabilities. I bring the business problem, the options compared, the brief and the measures.

  5. Red flag 05Control shown

    Adoption that fades after the enthusiasts

    The concern

    “The enthusiasts will love it. Everyone else will drift back to the old way.”

    My answer

    I’d find the champions first. At Mattick Air a well-received group session didn’t change how most people worked, so I switched to one-to-ones on their own work and tracked uptake person by person. At Ailo, an enablement platform I built had 83% monthly use across its 19 users.

    The control

    A check a few months after launch on who’s still using it, person by person, reported to the business owner.

  6. Red flag 06Control shown

    Influence with executives and governance forums

    The concern

    “Can he hold his own with our executives and governance forums?”

    My answer

    At Ailo I owned about $150,000 a year in sales and marketing software, and led the company-wide Salesforce to HubSpot move. I spent six years on the Asia Pacific advisory council of Public Interest Registry (.org). I’m used to putting a recommendation to people who can say no.

    The control

    Each recommendation reaches the forum with its expected value, measures and risks written down, so the decision stays with the forum.
